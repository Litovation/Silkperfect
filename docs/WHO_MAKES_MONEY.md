# Who makes money from trading in India, and how

Written 12 Nov 2026. Plain language on purpose. Figures come from SEBI
studies and press reports; where a number is disputed or unconfirmed, it
says so. This is background for the Silkperfect project, not financial advice.

## The short version

- Yes, some people make very large, steady profits from trading in India.
  They are professional firms, not individuals at home.
- Most of that money is made in **options on the Nifty and Bank Nifty**,
  not in ordinary shares.
- They do not win by guessing where prices will go. They win by being the
  shop that everyone else trades with, and by being faster than everyone else.
- The money they make is, to a large extent, the money ordinary traders lose.
- None of their methods can be copied from a laptop. The realistic choices
  for an ordinary person are different, and are listed at the end.

## The numbers, checked

| Fact | Figure | How sure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Profit of trading firms' own desks ("proprietary traders") from futures and options, year to March 2024 | about Rs 33,000 crore, before costs | SEBI study, September 2024 |
| Profit of foreign funds from the same, same year | about Rs 28,000 crore, before costs | Same study |
| Share of those profits made by computer programs | 97% for foreign funds, 96% for proprietary desks | Same study |
| Individuals who lost money in futures and options, 2021-22 to 2023-24 | 93% | Same study |
| Individuals who lost money, year to March 2025 | about 91% | SEBI study, July 2025 |
| What individuals lost in total, year to March 2025 | about Rs 1.05 lakh crore after costs | Same study; a later SEBI revision is reported as Rs 1.12 lakh crore |
| Average loss per individual, year to March 2025 | about Rs 1.1 lakh | Press reports of the study |

Put side by side: in one year the professionals made roughly Rs 61,000 crore
before costs, and individuals lost a similar or larger amount. It is close to
a transfer from one group to the other, with brokers, exchanges and the tax
office taking fees on the way.

## Who the winners are

- **Indian firms:** Graviton Research Capital, Quadeye, AlphaGrep, NK Securities.
- **Foreign firms:** Jane Street, Citadel Securities, Hudson River Trading,
  Optiver, IMC, and Tower Research (its Indian arm is called Shastra).
- Many sit in the same office district in Gurugram. They hire top engineering
  and mathematics graduates and pay them very highly.
- How much each one earns is mostly private. Claims such as "the top eight
  firms take 75% of the profit" appear in the press but are not confirmed.

## How they make money: four methods

### 1. Being the shopkeeper (market making)

Think of a currency-exchange counter at an airport. It buys dollars at Rs 83
and sells them at Rs 84. It does not care whether the dollar rises or falls
next week. It earns the one-rupee gap, thousands of times a day.

Trading firms do the same with options. At every moment they offer to buy at
one price and sell at a slightly higher one. Each trade earns a few paise.
They do this millions of times a day.

- Their risk is holding too much of something when the price moves, so their
  programs constantly balance what they hold.
- This is the largest and steadiest source of profit, and index options are
  where most of it happens.

### 2. Being first (speed)

When news arrives or one price moves, related prices must follow. Whoever
reacts first buys the item that has not caught up yet and sells it a moment
later.

- "First" here means millionths of a second.
- To be that fast, firms rent space for their computers inside the stock
  exchange's own building (this is called co-location), use special hardware,
  and write their own software.
- This costs crores a year. An ordinary internet connection is thousands of
  times slower, so an ordinary trader is always last in this race.

### 3. Same thing, two prices (arbitrage)

The Nifty index is just 50 shares added together. The Nifty future and Nifty
options are priced off it. Sometimes the pieces and the whole disagree by a
tiny amount. A firm buys the cheaper one and sells the dearer one at the same
instant, and keeps the difference whatever the market does next.

- The gaps are tiny and vanish in a fraction of a second.
- It needs speed, very low trading costs and a lot of money.

### 4. Selling insurance (option selling)

An option works like an insurance policy on the market. Buyers pay a small
fee hoping for a big payout. Most policies expire without paying out.

- Individuals mostly **buy** these, especially on the day they expire,
  hoping to turn a small sum into a large one. It is close to buying lottery
  tickets.
- Professional firms mostly end up on the other side, collecting the fees,
  and use their programs to protect themselves on the rare days the payout
  is large.
- Over a year the seller of lottery tickets earns more than the buyers.

## Why India in particular

- India became the world's largest options market by number of contracts.
- Very many individuals trade, in small sizes, with weekly expiry dates that
  encouraged frequent bets.
- Costs for professional firms are low, and until recently limits were loose.
- That is a large, steady flow of small losing bets for the shopkeepers to
  take the other side of.

SEBI has responded since late 2024: fewer weekly expiries, bigger minimum
contract sizes, more money required up front. Fewer individuals now trade
options (about 61 lakh at the start of 2024-25 falling to about 43 lakh by
its end), but those who remain still mostly lose.

## The Jane Street case: when a method crosses the line

- In July 2025 SEBI barred the American firm Jane Street and ordered it to
  set aside Rs 4,843.57 crore that SEBI called unlawful gains.
- SEBI's claim: on the days Bank Nifty options expired, the firm bought
  Bank Nifty shares heavily in the morning, pushing the index up, while
  holding much larger options positions that profited when it later sold
  the shares and the index fell back.
- Jane Street's reply: this was ordinary arbitrage, and it did nothing wrong.
  It deposited the money while it contests the case.
- Status: not decided. As of late October 2026 the case was before the
  Securities Appellate Tribunal, with a ruling expected within a couple of
  months. Whether the firm has returned to trading in India is not confirmed
  by the sources checked.
- The lesson: even for the biggest firms, the rule-makers are a real risk.

## China and the rest of the world, briefly

- **China:** large funds such as High-Flyer, Ubiquant and Minghong trade
  thousands of shares using computer models built on price and volume. They
  had a bad 2024 after the regulator clamped down in February of that year.
  Reported returns for 2025 are unconfirmed.
- **United States:** Renaissance Technologies' Medallion fund is the most
  famous, reported at roughly 66% a year before its fees over decades. It is
  closed to outside investors and its methods are secret. Two Sigma's main
  fund is reported to have made about 11% in 2024, which is what a good year
  looks like for a large fund open to clients.

## What they have that a person at home does not

| They have | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Computers inside the exchange building | Their orders arrive first |
| Trading costs close to zero | A profit of a few paise per trade is still a profit |
| Crores of capital and borrowed money | Tiny margins add up only at huge size |
| Teams of mathematicians and engineers | Programs are rewritten constantly as edges fade |
| Millions of trades a day | Luck averages out; a small edge becomes steady income |
| Permission to act as market makers | Access and fee terms ordinary accounts do not get |

This project's own tests show the same thing from the other side. With an
ordinary account's costs:

- Day trading a breakout rule lost in all 48 versions tested.
- Three AI forecasting models and Microsoft's Qlib recipe all did worse than
  simply holding every share.
- Trading more often made every method worse, because costs grow with each
  trade while the gain does not.

## What is left for an ordinary person, in order of preference

1. **A low-cost index fund, held for years.** Owning the whole market means
   earning what businesses earn, with almost no fees and no need to beat
   anyone. This is the base case everything else has to beat.
2. **A momentum fund, as a part of the money.** It follows the one idea that
   held up in this project's tests: hold the shares that have risen most,
   swapped twice a year. One purchase, low fees. It can fall harder than the
   market in bad stretches.
3. **Momentum done by hand, only with larger sums.** The dashboard's pretend
   account follows this. It needs roughly Rs 1 lakh or more, swaps only every
   three to six months, and several months of pretend results first.
4. **Avoid: buying options, day trading, and paid "algo" strategies.** These
   are the activities that feed the professionals described above. No verified
   record was found of an individual making steady money from a rented
   trading robot.
5. **If the goal is to earn from these methods rather than fight them:** the
   route is a job at one of these firms, which hire strong programmers and
   mathematicians. That is a career path, not an investment.
